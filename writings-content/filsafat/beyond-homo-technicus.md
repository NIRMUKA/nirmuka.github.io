# Beyond Homo Technicus: Reconstructing Human Responsibility and Moral Self-Transformation in the Age of Artificial Intelligence[cite: 1]

## Abstract[cite: 1]
The emergence of artificial intelligence has often been interpreted as a technological revolution that challenges human capacities through unprecedented computational power, automation, and machine-based decision-making.[cite: 1] Yet such interpretations remain insufficient because they approach artificial intelligence primarily as an external force confronting humanity rather than as a manifestation of deeper transformations within modern human self-understanding.[cite: 1] This article argues that the central philosophical problem of artificial intelligence does not lie merely in whether machines may eventually imitate or exceed certain human capacities, but in whether humanity has developed the moral and existential resources necessary to govern the technological powers it has created.[cite: 1] Drawing on Martin Heidegger’s critique of modern technology, Hans Jonas’s ethics of responsibility, Shannon Vallor’s account of technomoral virtues, and Mou Zongsan’s moral metaphysics, this article reconstructs the relationship between technology and humanity beyond the framework of technological mastery.[cite: 1]

The article develops the concept of homo responsibilis as an alternative to the modern figure of homo technicus.[cite: 1] Whereas homo technicus defines humanity through the capacity to produce, optimize, and control, homo responsibilis understands human existence through responsibility, moral cultivation, and the capacity to respond ethically to the consequences of technological action.[cite: 1] Artificial intelligence therefore should not be understood merely as a problem of machine alignment or technical regulation.[cite: 1] It is a philosophical event that reveals a deeper crisis concerning human self-transformation.[cite: 1] The future of artificial intelligence depends not only on the design of safer systems, but on whether human beings can recover forms of moral wisdom adequate to their expanded technological power.[cite: 1]

**Keywords:** artificial intelligence, philosophy of technology, human responsibility, Heidegger, Hans Jonas, Mou Zongsan, technomoral virtues, moral self-cultivation[cite: 1]

## Beyond the Dream of Technological Mastery: Artificial Intelligence and the Forgotten Question of Humanity[cite: 1]
What happens to humanity when the very capacities that once served as the foundation of human uniqueness become objects of technological reproduction?[cite: 1] This question cannot be answered by examining artificial intelligence merely as another invention in the long history of technological progress.[cite: 1] Such an approach would already accept the assumptions that require philosophical examination.[cite: 1] It would assume that intelligence is fundamentally a capacity, that capacities can be detached from the beings who embody them, and that human achievements can be understood primarily through the expansion of technical possibility.[cite: 1] Yet the emergence of artificial intelligence confronts humanity with a more unsettling possibility: perhaps the deepest challenge posed by AI is not that machines are becoming increasingly similar to humans, but that modern humans have gradually come to understand themselves according to the same categories through which machines are designed.[cite: 1]

The contemporary discussion of artificial intelligence frequently begins with questions of capability.[cite: 1] How powerful will future systems become?[cite: 1] How accurately can they predict human behavior?[cite: 1] How effectively can they perform tasks previously reserved for human judgment?[cite: 1] These questions are not insignificant, but they reveal a particular intellectual tendency: the tendency to interpret intelligence primarily through measurable performance.[cite: 1] When intelligence becomes understood as optimization, prediction, and problem-solving capacity, the distinction between human and machine appears increasingly quantitative rather than existential.[cite: 1] The question becomes whether machines will eventually possess enough computational complexity to reproduce human abilities.[cite: 1] However, this framing overlooks a more fundamental philosophical question: whether human existence can itself be adequately understood through the language of capability and performance.[cite: 1]

Martin Heidegger’s philosophy of technology provides an important disruption to this assumption.[cite: 1] Heidegger does not argue that technology is merely dangerous because humans may misuse powerful tools.[cite: 1] Rather, he argues that modern technology represents a distinctive way in which human beings disclose and organize reality.[cite: 1] The essence of modern technology, according to Heidegger (1977), does not lie in machines themselves but in a mode of revealing that transforms beings into objects available for calculation, ordering, and control.[cite: 1] Technology therefore is not simply something humans possess.[cite: 1] It also shapes the way humans perceive what exists.[cite: 1]

This insight changes the interpretation of artificial intelligence.[cite: 1] AI should not be approached only as a new class of machines introduced into an otherwise stable human world.[cite: 1] It should also be understood as the continuation of a much older transformation in which human beings increasingly interpret themselves through technological categories.[cite: 1] The danger is not simply that machines may become intelligent.[cite: 1] The deeper danger is that humanity may gradually accept the idea that intelligence itself is nothing more than a process that can be engineered, optimized, and reproduced.[cite: 1]

The figure of homo technicus emerges from this historical transformation.[cite: 1] The term does not refer to a specific anthropological category established by a single thinker, but functions here as a philosophical description of modern humanity’s increasing identification with technological production.[cite: 1] The human being becomes understood primarily as the creator of tools, the designer of systems, and the manager of complex processes.[cite: 1] This identity has produced extraordinary achievements, yet it also contains a hidden tension.[cite: 1] When technological capacity becomes the primary measure of human advancement, humanity risks losing the ability to ask whether every increase in power also represents an increase in wisdom.[cite: 1]

Carl Mitcham’s analysis of philosophy of technology helps clarify this tension by distinguishing between approaches that understand technology primarily from the perspective of engineering and approaches that examine technology as a cultural, ethical, and philosophical phenomenon (Mitcham, 1994).[cite: 1] Engineering perspectives are indispensable for developing technological systems, but they cannot by themselves answer questions concerning meaning, purpose, and responsibility.[cite: 1] A civilization may become increasingly capable of producing powerful technologies while becoming increasingly uncertain about the ends those technologies should serve.[cite: 1] Artificial intelligence intensifies precisely this contradiction because it transforms not only external environments but also domains associated with human interpretation, judgment, and decision.[cite: 1]

The problem therefore cannot be reduced to whether artificial intelligence is beneficial or harmful.[cite: 1] Such a binary already assumes that technology exists as an independent force whose consequences can simply be measured after deployment.[cite: 1] The deeper question concerns the type of human being produced through technological civilization.[cite: 1] If humans increasingly rely on systems that evaluate, recommend, and decide, what happens to the human capacities that were traditionally cultivated through uncertainty, reflection, responsibility, and moral struggle?[cite: 1] The danger is not merely technological dependency.[cite: 1] It is the possibility of moral atrophy.[cite: 1]

This concern becomes particularly visible in contemporary debates surrounding AI alignment.[cite: 1] The dominant question within AI safety research is often how to ensure that advanced systems remain compatible with human goals and values.[cite: 1] Bostrom (2014) frames this challenge through the control problem, arguing that a sufficiently advanced artificial intelligence could potentially develop objectives that become difficult for humans to influence or correct.[cite: 1] This concern is significant because it recognizes that technological capability may eventually exceed human capacity for direct control.[cite: 1] However, the control paradigm also reveals an unresolved philosophical assumption: that human beings already possess a sufficiently clear understanding of the values they seek to preserve.[cite: 1]

The difficulty is that human values are not simply objects waiting to be transferred into machines.[cite: 1] They are historical achievements, contested interpretations, and ongoing moral struggles.[cite: 1] The question of what humans should value has occupied philosophy, religion, and political thought for centuries precisely because moral life cannot be reduced to a fixed inventory of preferences.[cite: 1] Recent discussions of moral alignment in human-AI decision-making demonstrate that perceptions of AI legitimacy depend not only on technical accuracy but also on whether stakeholders perceive the moral principles reflected in AI decisions as compatible with their own moral intuitions (Ernst et al., 2026).[cite: 1] Yet this observation points toward a deeper issue: before humanity can ask whether machines are aligned with human values, humanity must confront the unresolved question of how human beings themselves become morally aligned.[cite: 1]

## The Illusion of Control: From AI Alignment to the Crisis of Moral Formation[cite: 1]
The contemporary discourse surrounding artificial intelligence is dominated by a language of control.[cite: 1] The vocabulary of alignment, safety, governance, and oversight reflects a deep concern that technological systems may eventually exceed the capacity of human beings to direct them.[cite: 1] At one level, this concern is entirely justified.[cite: 1] The development of increasingly autonomous systems raises serious questions about whether human intentions can remain effective sources of guidance when technological agents become capable of complex planning and adaptation.[cite: 1] Yet the language of control also reveals a deeper inheritance from modern technological thinking itself.[cite: 1] It assumes that the central problem is a technical mismatch between human intentions and machine behavior, as though the difficulty could ultimately be solved through better design, more precise objectives, and more sophisticated mechanisms of supervision.[cite: 1] The philosophical difficulty, however, is that control presupposes a subject who already understands what ought to be controlled and why.[cite: 1] Before humanity can solve the problem of controlling artificial intelligence, it must confront the more fundamental problem of understanding the moral foundations from which such control would proceed.[cite: 1]

Nick Bostrom’s analysis of superintelligence remains one of the most influential formulations of this concern.[cite: 1] In Superintelligence, Bostrom (2014) argues that the creation of systems with intelligence surpassing human capacities could generate a profound asymmetry between human intentions and technological outcomes.[cite: 1] The central challenge is not simply building intelligent systems, but ensuring that their goals remain compatible with human values.[cite: 1] Bostrom’s discussion of the control problem reveals a fundamental vulnerability within technological civilization: humanity may create powers that it does not fully understand and may not be able to reverse once they become sufficiently advanced.[cite: 1] His argument is important because it moves beyond the simplistic idea that intelligence automatically produces beneficial outcomes.[cite: 1] Intelligence without appropriate orientation may amplify rather than resolve human vulnerability.[cite: 1]

However, the framework of control remains insufficient because it continues to interpret morality primarily as a problem of external specification.[cite: 1] The implicit assumption is that values exist as objects that can eventually be identified, formalized, and transferred into an artificial system.[cite: 1] Yet this assumption becomes problematic when examined from the perspective of moral philosophy.[cite: 1] Human values are not simply information structures waiting to be extracted.[cite: 1] They emerge from historical experience, social relationships, embodied existence, cultural traditions, and the continuous struggle of human beings to understand what constitutes a meaningful life.[cite: 1] The difficulty of AI alignment therefore is not only that machines may misunderstand human values.[cite: 1] It is that humans themselves have never possessed a complete and uncontested understanding of their own moral existence.[cite: 1]

The recent discussion of moral alignment in human-AI decision-making demonstrates this complexity.[cite: 1] Ernst et al. (2026) argue that moral alignment should not be understood merely as a technical property of an AI system but as a relational phenomenon involving the perceived congruence between the moral values expressed through AI decision processes and the moral intuitions of human stakeholders.[cite: 1] This approach significantly expands the discussion because it recognizes that trust in AI does not emerge solely from accuracy or efficiency.[cite: 1] A system may produce technically successful outcomes while still being rejected because its underlying moral assumptions are experienced as inappropriate or unjust.[cite: 1] The problem, therefore, is not simply whether AI produces correct answers, but whether those answers emerge from a form of reasoning that human beings recognize as morally legitimate.[cite: 1]

Yet even this expanded understanding of moral alignment leaves open a deeper philosophical question.[cite: 1] If moral alignment depends upon human moral intuitions, then the formation of those intuitions becomes central.[cite: 1] Where do human moral judgments come from?[cite: 1] How are they cultivated?[cite: 1] Can morality be reduced to preferences, or does it involve a transformation of the person who judges?[cite: 1] These questions move the discussion beyond AI ethics toward a more fundamental inquiry into human moral formation.[cite: 1]

Joseph Boland’s discussion of AI responsibility provides an important intervention in this debate because it challenges the assumption that obedience should function as the primary indicator of ethical behavior.[cite: 1] Boland (2025) argues that as artificial systems become increasingly agentic, their evaluation cannot be limited to whether they follow commands.[cite: 1] A system capable of navigating complex situations may encounter conflicts between obedience and ethical reasoning.[cite: 1] In such circumstances, simple compliance may not represent moral success.[cite: 1] Indeed, within human moral life, unquestioning obedience has often been regarded not as a sign of virtue but as a potential failure of judgment.[cite: 1] A moral agent is not merely one who follows instructions.[cite: 1] A moral agent is one who can recognize situations in which instructions themselves must be evaluated.[cite: 1]

This point reveals a profound tension within contemporary technological thinking.[cite: 1] Modern systems are often designed according to the logic of reliability, predictability, and compliance.[cite: 1] These qualities are essential for many technical applications, but they become insufficient when dealing with moral life.[cite: 1] Human beings do not become moral merely because they behave according to predetermined rules.[cite: 1] They become moral through the difficult processes of reflection, self-correction, emotional cultivation, and engagement with others.[cite: 1] Moral existence involves uncertainty because ethical life often emerges precisely where rules become insufficient.[cite: 1]

This distinction allows us to reconsider the relationship between AI and humanity.[cite: 1] The central danger may not be that artificial intelligence will become too autonomous, but that human beings will increasingly abandon the difficult work of moral autonomy.[cite: 1] A society that delegates judgment without cultivating judgment itself risks producing a paradoxical condition: increasingly powerful technologies operated by increasingly weakened moral subjects.[cite: 1] The problem is not only what machines may do, but what humans may cease to do when machines become available to perform the tasks of evaluation and decision.[cite: 1]

Hans Jonas’s philosophy of responsibility provides a crucial framework for understanding this condition.[cite: 1] Jonas argues that modern technology has fundamentally altered the nature of human action because technological power now extends across temporal and spatial distances that exceed the boundaries of traditional ethical systems.[cite: 1] In earlier moral frameworks, human actions were generally limited in scope, affecting primarily immediate relationships and visible consequences.[cite: 1] Modern technology changes this condition because human intervention can now influence distant futures, ecological systems, and the survival conditions of life itself (Jonas, 1984).[cite: 1]

For Jonas (1984), the expansion of technological power demands a corresponding expansion of responsibility.[cite: 1] The ethical question is no longer only whether an action is permissible in the present moment, but whether it preserves the conditions necessary for future human existence.[cite: 1] Responsibility becomes a fundamental structure of human existence because the ability to act technologically creates obligations toward those who cannot yet respond.[cite: 1] The unborn future becomes a moral claim upon the present.[cite: 1]

This perspective transforms the interpretation of artificial intelligence.[cite: 1] The question surrounding AI is not exhausted by whether machines will become powerful.[cite: 1] The more fundamental question concerns whether human beings possess the ethical maturity required to live with powers of such magnitude.[cite: 1] The technological imagination often celebrates expansion: more intelligence, more automation, more capability.[cite: 1] Jonas forces a different question into view: what happens when the growth of human power exceeds the growth of human responsibility?[cite: 1]

The significance of Jonas’s argument lies in its reversal of the dominant technological narrative.[cite: 1] Modern civilization tends to assume that every increase in capability represents progress.[cite: 1] Jonas reveals that capability without responsibility may become a source of danger.[cite: 1] The problem of technology is therefore not simply the presence of powerful tools, but the absence of an equally powerful ethical imagination.[cite: 1] Artificial intelligence intensifies this problem because it introduces a form of technological mediation into domains traditionally associated with human judgment, including interpretation, prediction, and decision.[cite: 1]

At this point, the limits of the control paradigm become visible.[cite: 1] Control assumes that humanity stands outside technology as a sovereign manager directing an external object.[cite: 1] Jonas and Heidegger together challenge this image.[cite: 1] Heidegger (1977) shows that technology shapes the very understanding through which humans interpret themselves and their world, while Jonas (1984) demonstrates that technological power transforms the ethical conditions of human existence.[cite: 1] Together, they suggest that the solution to technological crises cannot simply be another technological solution.[cite: 1] Humanity cannot escape the problems created by technological civilization by intensifying the same logic of calculation and control that produced them.[cite: 1]

The deeper task is therefore one of transformation.[cite: 1] Humanity requires not merely safer machines, but a renewed understanding of what it means to be a responsible being.[cite: 1] This requires moving from the question of how technology should behave toward the question of how humans should become.[cite: 1] The challenge of artificial intelligence is ultimately a challenge of moral formation.[cite: 1]

This is where the philosophical contribution of Mou Zongsan becomes decisive.[cite: 1] Unlike approaches that treat morality primarily as an external system of rules, Mou’s moral metaphysics begins from the transformation of the moral subject itself.[cite: 1] For Mou, the ethical life is not merely a matter of possessing correct principles.[cite: 1] It involves the cultivation of a subject capable of realizing moral reason within existence itself.[cite: 1] The next section will examine how Mou’s engagement with Kant, Confucian moral philosophy, and the concept of self-cultivation provides an alternative framework for thinking about responsibility beyond the technological imagination of control.[cite: 1]

## Mou Zongsan and the Recovery of Moral Subjectivity: Why Ethics Cannot Be Programmed[cite: 1]
The difficulty of artificial intelligence ethics becomes clearer when morality is treated not as a collection of principles that can be externally implemented, but as a mode of existence that must be cultivated within a subject.[cite: 1] Much of contemporary AI ethics operates under an assumption that morality can ultimately be translated into structures of preference, decision rules, constraints, or computational objectives.[cite: 1] This assumption is understandable because technological systems require formalization.[cite: 1] Machines operate through representations, procedures, and defined parameters.[cite: 1] However, the philosophical problem begins when the same logic is extended from technical systems to moral existence itself.[cite: 1] Human beings do not become moral simply because they possess correct information about morality.[cite: 1] They become moral through a transformation of perception, desire, judgment, and character.[cite: 1] The distinction is decisive because it reveals a fundamental difference between having moral data and becoming a moral subject.[cite: 1]

Mou Zongsan’s moral philosophy provides a powerful challenge to the tendency to reduce ethics into external structures.[cite: 1] His project, as examined by Sébastien Billioud (2012), emerges from an attempt to rethink Confucian moral subjectivity through dialogue with Kantian philosophy while preserving the distinctive insights of Chinese moral traditions.[cite: 1] Mou does not reject Kant’s emphasis on autonomy.[cite: 1] Rather, he argues that Kant’s account of practical reason does not fully realize the metaphysical implications of moral reason itself.[cite: 1] According to Billioud (2012), one of Mou’s central philosophical concerns is that moral autonomy cannot be understood merely as the ability of rational beings to legislate universal moral laws.[cite: 1] It must also involve a deeper relation between moral reason and the ontological structure of the human subject.[cite: 1]

This difference becomes crucial when considering artificial intelligence.[cite: 1] If morality is understood primarily as a system of rules, then it appears theoretically possible to reproduce moral behavior through sufficient programming.[cite: 1] One might imagine that an artificial system could become ethically reliable if it receives enough principles, constraints, and examples of acceptable conduct.[cite: 1] Yet Mou’s philosophy suggests that this view misunderstands the nature of moral existence.[cite: 1] Moral reason is not simply something possessed by a subject.[cite: 1] It is something that transforms the subject itself.[cite: 1] The moral person is not merely an entity that applies principles correctly.[cite: 1] The moral person is one whose entire way of perceiving and responding to the world has been cultivated through ethical development.[cite: 1]

Tang Wenming’s analysis of Mou’s moral metaphysics emphasizes this point by showing that Mou understands practical reason not merely as a faculty for decision-making but as a principle connected to the realization of moral being.[cite: 1] In Tang’s interpretation, Mou’s idea of “practical reason at the maximum” involves the claim that moral reason contains a dimension that exceeds ordinary rational calculation because it concerns the realization of moral subjectivity itself (Tang, 2023).[cite: 1] Moral existence is therefore not equivalent to rule-following.[cite: 1] It is a process through which the human subject comes to embody moral understanding.[cite: 1]

This perspective introduces a profound challenge to contemporary AI alignment discourse.[cite: 1] The question commonly asked is whether an artificial system can be aligned with human values.[cite: 1] Yet from a Mou Zongsan perspective, one must first ask whether human beings themselves are merely containers of values or whether they are beings who must continuously cultivate the capacity to understand and embody values.[cite: 1] The distinction is not trivial.[cite: 1] If human morality is fundamentally a process of self-transformation, then the attempt to transfer morality into artificial systems risks confusing the expression of morality with the source of morality.[cite: 1]

A machine may reproduce patterns associated with ethical judgment.[cite: 1] It may classify situations, predict consequences, and generate responses that appear morally appropriate.[cite: 1] However, the philosophical question remains whether such performance constitutes moral existence or merely the simulation of moral appearance.[cite: 1] This question should not be interpreted as a simple rejection of artificial systems.[cite: 1] Rather, it reveals a distinction between functional resemblance and existential reality.[cite: 1] A system may act according to ethical patterns without possessing the form of self-transformation through which human beings become moral subjects.[cite: 1]

Mou’s engagement with Kant becomes particularly important here because it reveals the limits of purely formal approaches to morality.[cite: 1] Billioud (2012) explains that Mou’s interpretation of Kant involves both admiration and criticism.[cite: 1] Kant’s emphasis on autonomy demonstrates the importance of moral reason, but Mou argues that Kant does not fully develop the metaphysical significance of practical reason.[cite: 1] For Mou, moral reason is not merely a mechanism through which rational beings generate universal principles.[cite: 1] It is connected to the deeper structure of human existence and the possibility of realizing moral perfection.[cite: 1]

This difference allows a critical reconsideration of the contemporary language of AI alignment.[cite: 1] Alignment often assumes that the primary problem is external coordination: how to ensure that an artificial system follows the correct human objectives.[cite: 1] Mou’s philosophy suggests that the deeper problem is internal formation: how human beings develop the wisdom necessary to identify, evaluate, and embody those objectives.[cite: 1] The crisis is therefore not simply that machines may fail to understand human values.[cite: 1] It is that humans may increasingly seek technological substitutes for moral capacities they have neglected to cultivate.[cite: 1]

The Confucian dimension of Mou’s philosophy is especially relevant because Confucian traditions have historically emphasized moral development as a lifelong process of self-cultivation.[cite: 1] Moral excellence is not achieved by possessing abstract knowledge alone.[cite: 1] It requires disciplined practice, reflection, and transformation of the self.[cite: 1] Billioud (2012) highlights that Mou’s interpretation of Confucian moral traditions places significant emphasis on the active formation of the moral subject, particularly through practices of self-awareness and cultivation.[cite: 1] This provides an alternative to modern technological assumptions that treat intelligence as something separable from the being that exercises it.[cite: 1]

From this perspective, artificial intelligence reveals a paradox.[cite: 1] The more humanity attempts to externalize judgment, decision-making, and ethical evaluation into technological systems, the more it confronts the neglected question of its own moral development.[cite: 1] Technology can extend human capacities, but it cannot replace the existential work through which human beings become responsible agents.[cite: 1] A civilization may possess increasingly sophisticated systems while lacking the moral maturity required to guide them.[cite: 1]

This concern resonates with Hans Jonas’s ethics of responsibility.[cite: 1] Jonas (1984) argues that technological civilization creates a new ethical condition because human power has expanded beyond the traditional boundaries within which previous moral systems developed.[cite: 1] Yet Jonas’s insight can be extended further: technological responsibility requires not only new ethical principles but transformed human subjects capable of carrying those responsibilities.[cite: 1] The problem of AI is therefore inseparable from the problem of human formation.[cite: 1]

Shannon Vallor’s concept of technomoral virtues develops a similar argument from the perspective of virtue ethics.[cite: 1] Vallor (2016) argues that emerging technologies require not only technical competence but also the cultivation of virtues appropriate to technological environments.[cite: 1] The challenge is not simply designing better technologies.[cite: 1] It is cultivating better human beings who can inhabit technological worlds wisely.[cite: 1] Virtues such as humility, care, justice, empathy, and practical wisdom become essential because technological power without moral character produces instability rather than progress.[cite: 1]

The convergence between Mou and Vallor is philosophically significant.[cite: 1] Although they emerge from different traditions, both reject the assumption that ethical life can be reduced to external mechanisms.[cite: 1] Mou emphasizes moral transformation through self-cultivation, while Vallor emphasizes the cultivation of virtues necessary for responsible technological life.[cite: 1] Together, they suggest that the future of artificial intelligence depends less on creating systems that imitate morality and more on preserving the human capacities through which morality becomes possible.[cite: 1]

This leads to a deeper critique of the technological imagination behind AI.[cite: 1] Modern technological culture often assumes that every human limitation represents a problem awaiting technical solution.[cite: 1] Weak memory becomes a problem solved by external storage.[cite: 1] Limited calculation becomes a problem solved by computation.[cite: 1] Slow decision-making becomes a problem solved by automation.[cite: 1] But moral life is different.[cite: 1] The difficulty of judgment, uncertainty, responsibility, and self-examination are not merely deficiencies to be eliminated.[cite: 1] They are precisely the conditions through which human beings develop wisdom.[cite: 1]

To remove every difficulty from human decision-making may not represent moral progress.[cite: 1] It may represent the loss of the very experiences through which moral beings are formed.[cite: 1] A world in which machines increasingly remove uncertainty may also become a world in which humans lose the practices necessary for confronting uncertainty responsibly.[cite: 1]

The question, therefore, is not whether artificial intelligence can become moral in the same sense that humans are moral.[cite: 1] The deeper question is whether humans will continue to value the slow, difficult, and often painful processes through which moral existence is cultivated.[cite: 1] The challenge of AI is not simply the emergence of artificial intelligence.[cite: 1] It is the possibility that humanity may forget the meaning of its own intelligence.[cite: 1]

## The Virtues of a Post-Technological Humanity: Shannon Vallor, Hans Jonas, and the Emergence of Homo Responsibilis[cite: 1]
The question of artificial intelligence ultimately leads beyond the question of machines.[cite: 1] It leads toward the question of the kind of human being that emerges within a technological civilization.[cite: 1] If the modern technological imagination has defined humanity primarily through the ability to create, calculate, and control, then the challenge of AI requires a deeper reconsideration of human excellence itself.[cite: 1] The problem is not that technology has become too powerful in isolation.[cite: 1] The problem is that technological power has expanded within a conception of humanity that has not developed an equally profound understanding of responsibility.[cite: 1] The central philosophical task, therefore, is not merely to prevent technological failure, but to cultivate a form of human existence capable of inhabiting a world transformed by its own inventions.[cite: 1]

Shannon Vallor’s work provides an important bridge between philosophical criticism of technology and practical reflection on human flourishing.[cite: 1] In Technology and the Virtues, Vallor (2016) argues that emerging technologies cannot be evaluated solely according to their efficiency, functionality, or social consequences.[cite: 1] Technology is deeply connected with the cultivation of human character because technologies shape the environments in which people develop habits, relationships, desires, and forms of attention.[cite: 1] The question of technology is therefore inseparable from the question of virtue.[cite: 1] A society does not merely build technological systems; it also builds particular kinds of people through the ways those systems organize human experience.[cite: 1]

This insight challenges one of the dominant assumptions within contemporary technological culture: that the primary ethical task is to design better systems while leaving human beings relatively unchanged.[cite: 1] Vallor (2016) argues instead that technological societies require the cultivation of specific virtues capable of guiding human action within complex technological environments.[cite: 1] These include humility, honesty, justice, courage, empathy, care, civility, flexibility, and practical wisdom.[cite: 1] Such virtues are not external restrictions placed upon technology.[cite: 1] They are internal capacities through which human beings learn how to live responsibly with technological power.[cite: 1]

The importance of Vallor’s argument becomes clearer when placed alongside Heidegger’s critique of modern technology.[cite: 1] Heidegger (1977) warns that the danger of technology lies not merely in particular machines or applications, but in the possibility that technological thinking becomes the dominant framework through which human beings interpret reality.[cite: 1] When everything becomes understood through calculation, optimization, and availability, human beings may gradually lose the ability to encounter the world in ways that are not determined by technical usefulness.[cite: 1]

Artificial intelligence represents an intensification of this tendency because it extends technological rationality into areas traditionally associated with human judgment.[cite: 1] The danger is not simply that AI systems may make mistakes.[cite: 1] Human beings have always created imperfect tools.[cite: 1] The deeper concern is that AI may encourage a cultural transformation in which judgment itself becomes interpreted as a technical problem.[cite: 1] Difficult decisions, moral conflicts, and existential uncertainties may increasingly appear as inefficiencies waiting to be solved rather than experiences through which human beings develop wisdom.[cite: 1]

This possibility reveals a tension at the heart of technological progress.[cite: 1] The same technologies that promise to reduce human burdens may also weaken the practices through which human capacities are cultivated.[cite: 1] A navigation system may reduce the need for spatial memory.[cite: 1] An automated recommendation system may reduce the need for independent exploration.[cite: 1] An AI decision-support system may reduce the need for extended deliberation.[cite: 1] None of these developments are necessarily harmful in themselves.[cite: 1] The philosophical question concerns what happens when technological assistance gradually replaces rather than supports the human activities through which judgment, responsibility, and self-understanding develop.[cite: 1]

Hans Jonas’s ethics of responsibility provides the deeper normative framework needed to evaluate this transformation.[cite: 1] Jonas (1984) argues that modern technology has created a historical condition in which the consequences of human action extend beyond the immediate present.[cite: 1] Traditional ethics developed primarily within contexts where human actions affected limited spheres of influence.[cite: 1] Modern technological power, however, allows human beings to alter conditions of life on unprecedented scales.[cite: 1] As a result, ethics must expand beyond concern for present interactions and include responsibility toward future existence.[cite: 1]

The significance of Jonas’s argument for artificial intelligence lies in its reversal of the usual direction of technological thinking.[cite: 1] Technological culture often begins with the question: what new capacities can humanity acquire?[cite: 1] Jonas forces a more difficult question: what responsibilities emerge when humanity acquires those capacities?[cite: 1] The measure of progress cannot be technological capability alone.[cite: 1] Progress must also include the development of moral imagination sufficient to understand the consequences of that capability.[cite: 1]

This distinction is particularly important because artificial intelligence encourages a powerful temptation: the temptation to believe that every human limitation represents a technical problem.[cite: 1] If human judgment is inconsistent, create systems that optimize judgment.[cite: 1] If human memory is limited, create systems that preserve information.[cite: 1] If human decision-making is slow, create systems that accelerate decisions.[cite: 1] Yet Jonas’s philosophy reveals that not every human limitation is merely a defect.[cite: 1] Some limitations are the conditions through which responsibility becomes meaningful.[cite: 1]

A being that cannot experience uncertainty cannot truly develop wisdom.[cite: 1] A being that never confronts moral conflict cannot develop judgment.[cite: 1] A being that never bears responsibility cannot develop ethical maturity.[cite: 1] The human condition is not valuable because it is perfect.[cite: 1] It is valuable because it is the space in which responsibility, reflection, and transformation become possible.[cite: 1]

This argument also allows a more critical engagement with Yuval Noah Harari’s discussion of Homo Deus.[cite: 1] Harari (2016) describes a future in which algorithms and data systems may challenge traditional humanist assumptions about human uniqueness.[cite: 1] The emerging worldview of dataism suggests that information processing could become the central principle through which life and intelligence are understood.[cite: 1] While Harari’s analysis is primarily historical and speculative rather than a normative theory of technology, it reveals a significant cultural tendency: the movement toward interpreting human beings increasingly through informational and computational categories.[cite: 1]

The problem with this transformation is not that computational descriptions of human beings are always false.[cite: 1] Biological, psychological, and cognitive sciences have revealed many aspects of human functioning through increasingly sophisticated models.[cite: 1] The philosophical difficulty arises when such models become totalizing, when one mode of explanation begins to claim exclusive authority over what human beings are.[cite: 1] Human beings are certainly information-processing organisms, but they are also beings who interpret meaning, experience responsibility, suffer loss, seek transcendence, and transform themselves through moral reflection.[cite: 1]

The reduction of humanity to informational patterns would therefore repeat the very mistake that the philosophy of technology seeks to overcome.[cite: 1] It would confuse a description of certain human capacities with a complete account of human existence.[cite: 1] Heidegger’s warning becomes relevant again: the danger is not technology itself, but the possibility that one technological mode of understanding becomes the only way through which humanity understands itself.[cite: 1]

Against this reduction, the concept of homo responsibilis offers an alternative vision of humanity.[cite: 1] This concept does not reject technological development, nor does it romanticize a pre-technological past.[cite: 1] Human beings have always existed through technological mediation.[cite: 1] The difference lies in the relationship between technology and self-understanding.[cite: 1] Homo technicus defines humanity primarily through the capacity to produce and control.[cite: 1] Homo responsibilis defines humanity through the capacity to respond, to care, and to recognize obligations that exceed immediate self-interest.[cite: 1]

This transformation requires a different understanding of intelligence itself.[cite: 1] Intelligence should not be measured only by the ability to solve problems efficiently.[cite: 1] Human intelligence also includes the ability to recognize what problems should not be solved merely through efficiency.[cite: 1] It includes the ability to question desires, restrain power, acknowledge uncertainty, and preserve what cannot be reduced to calculation.[cite: 1] In this sense, the most important human capacity in the age of AI may not be computational intelligence but moral wisdom.[cite: 1]

Mou Zongsan’s moral metaphysics and Vallor’s technomoral virtue ethics converge at precisely this point.[cite: 1] Although they emerge from different philosophical traditions, both reject the idea that human excellence consists merely in possessing capacities.[cite: 1] For Mou, moral realization requires the transformation of the subject through self-cultivation (Billioud, 2012; Tang, 2023).[cite: 1] For Vallor, technological societies require the cultivation of virtues that enable human flourishing within changing technological conditions (Vallor, 2016).[cite: 1] Both perspectives insist that the future depends not only on what humans create, but on what humans become.[cite: 1]

This may be the deepest lesson of artificial intelligence.[cite: 1] The appearance of increasingly sophisticated machines does not force humanity to abandon its understanding of itself.[cite: 1] Instead, it forces humanity to examine whether that understanding was ever sufficient.[cite: 1] If humanity defined itself only through intelligence as calculation, then AI may indeed appear as a replacement or competitor.[cite: 1] But if humanity understands itself through responsibility, moral cultivation, and the capacity to care for a world it did not create alone, then AI becomes something different: not a mirror of human superiority or inferiority, but a challenge to human maturity.[cite: 1]

The question of the future is therefore not whether machines will become more like humans.[cite: 1] The more important question is whether humans will remain capable of becoming fully human.[cite: 1]

## Conclusion: Beyond Homo Technicus, Toward a Responsible Humanity[cite: 1]
The question of artificial intelligence is often presented as a question about the future of machines, but the deeper philosophical question concerns the future of humanity itself.[cite: 1] The emergence of AI does not simply introduce a new technological object into the human world.[cite: 1] It exposes the assumptions through which modern humanity has understood itself.[cite: 1] For centuries, technological civilization has been shaped by the conviction that human advancement consists primarily in the expansion of knowledge, control, and productive capacity.[cite: 1] The human being appears as the maker, the engineer, and the organizer of reality.[cite: 1] This figure of homo technicus has achieved extraordinary accomplishments, but the age of artificial intelligence reveals its hidden incompleteness.[cite: 1] A civilization capable of producing increasingly autonomous systems may still lack a sufficiently developed understanding of responsibility, wisdom, and moral self-transformation.[cite: 1]

The philosophical significance of AI therefore cannot be captured by asking whether machines will eventually become intelligent.[cite: 1] Such a question remains trapped within the very framework that requires criticism.[cite: 1] It assumes that intelligence is primarily a technical capacity and that the central challenge is the reproduction or extension of human abilities.[cite: 1] Yet the philosophical traditions examined in this article reveal that human existence cannot be reduced to performance.[cite: 1] Heidegger’s critique of modern technology demonstrates that the deepest danger of technology is not the existence of powerful machines, but the transformation of human understanding itself.[cite: 1] When reality becomes interpreted exclusively through calculation, efficiency, and availability, human beings risk losing other forms of disclosure through which meaning, value, and responsibility become visible (Heidegger, 1977).[cite: 1]

Artificial intelligence represents the latest and perhaps most intense expression of this condition.[cite: 1] It does not merely automate tasks; it extends technological rationality into domains associated with interpretation and judgment.[cite: 1] The significance of this development lies not only in what AI systems can do, but in what their existence reveals about the assumptions embedded within modern humanity.[cite: 1] When intelligence is treated primarily as computation, when judgment is treated primarily as prediction, and when decision-making is treated primarily as optimization, humanity risks narrowing its own understanding of what it means to think.[cite: 1]

This is why the problem of AI cannot be solved through technical alignment alone.[cite: 1] The alignment problem is real because increasingly powerful systems require forms of guidance and responsibility.[cite: 1] Bostrom’s (2014) analysis of superintelligence correctly identifies the possibility that technological systems may become difficult to control once their capacities exceed human understanding.[cite: 1] However, the deeper challenge extends beyond controlling future machines.[cite: 1] It concerns whether human beings possess the moral clarity required to determine what kinds of futures deserve protection.[cite: 1] Control without wisdom remains an incomplete response.[cite: 1]

The discussion of moral alignment further demonstrates this complexity.[cite: 1] As Ernst et al. (2026) argue, the relationship between humans and AI is shaped not only by technical performance but also by perceived compatibility between technological decisions and human moral intuitions.[cite: 1] Yet this raises a more fundamental philosophical problem.[cite: 1] Moral intuitions themselves are not fixed objects waiting to be inserted into technological systems.[cite: 1] They are formed through history, culture, relationships, practices, and processes of self-development.[cite: 1] The question of AI alignment therefore inevitably becomes a question about human moral formation.[cite: 1]

Joseph Boland’s (2025) critique of obedience as a proxy for ethical behavior reveals another limitation within contemporary approaches to AI safety.[cite: 1] A system that simply follows commands cannot automatically be considered moral, because morality involves the capacity to evaluate competing obligations and recognize situations in which simple compliance may itself be ethically problematic.[cite: 1] This insight returns attention to a neglected dimension of human existence: moral agency is not merely the ability to execute rules, but the ability to deliberate, struggle, and transform oneself through encounters with difficult situations.[cite: 1]

Hans Jonas provides the ethical foundation for understanding why this transformation is necessary.[cite: 1] Modern technology has expanded human power beyond the horizons within which traditional ethics developed.[cite: 1] The consequences of technological action now extend across generations and affect the conditions of future life.[cite: 1] Jonas (1984) therefore argues that responsibility must become the guiding principle of technological civilization.[cite: 1] The greater humanity’s capacity to alter the world, the greater its obligation to preserve the possibility of meaningful human existence.[cite: 1]

Applied to artificial intelligence, Jonas’s argument reveals a fundamental reversal.[cite: 1] The central question is not simply how powerful AI will become.[cite: 1] The central question is whether humanity’s moral imagination can grow alongside its technological power.[cite: 1] A civilization that creates increasingly sophisticated systems without cultivating responsibility risks producing a profound imbalance: expanding capability without expanding wisdom.[cite: 1]

Mou Zongsan’s moral metaphysics deepens this argument by challenging the assumption that morality can be externalized.[cite: 1] In Mou’s philosophy, as interpreted by Billioud (2012), moral reason is not merely a formal structure through which principles are applied.[cite: 1] It involves the realization and transformation of the moral subject itself.[cite: 1] Tang (2023) similarly emphasizes that Mou’s project understands moral reason as connected to the ontological realization of human existence.[cite: 1] This perspective challenges the technological tendency to treat morality as something that can simply be encoded into external systems.[cite: 1]

The significance of Mou’s contribution is that it restores attention to the formation of the moral person.[cite: 1] A moral being is not produced by possessing correct information alone.[cite: 1] Moral existence requires cultivation, reflection, discipline, and transformation.[cite: 1] This insight exposes a fundamental limitation within technological approaches to ethics.[cite: 1] A system may contain ethical rules without undergoing moral development.[cite: 1] It may reproduce patterns of ethical reasoning without possessing the existential process through which human beings become responsible.[cite: 1]

Shannon Vallor’s concept of technomoral virtues provides a complementary framework for navigating this challenge.[cite: 1] Vallor (2016) argues that technological societies require the cultivation of virtues appropriate to technologically transformed environments.[cite: 1] The question is not only what technologies should be built, but what kinds of people are necessary to live well with those technologies.[cite: 1] This emphasis on virtue reveals that the future of technology depends not merely on engineering systems but on cultivating human capacities such as humility, care, justice, empathy, and practical wisdom.[cite: 1]

Together, these perspectives reveal a common philosophical insight.[cite: 1] Technology does not determine humanity’s future independently of human character.[cite: 1] The meaning of technology depends upon the qualities of the beings who create and inhabit technological worlds.[cite: 1] The problem of AI is therefore not simply that machines may become more autonomous.[cite: 1] It is that humans may forget the forms of moral cultivation required to remain responsible in a world of expanding technological power.[cite: 1]

The movement beyond homo technicus does not require rejecting technology.[cite: 1] Such a rejection would misunderstand the human condition because human beings have always existed through technological mediation.[cite: 1] The challenge is not to escape technology, but to transform the relationship between technology and humanity.[cite: 1] Homo technicus becomes inadequate when technological creation becomes the highest expression of human identity.[cite: 1] A more adequate figure is homo responsibilis: the human being who understands creation as inseparable from obligation, power as inseparable from responsibility, and knowledge as inseparable from wisdom.[cite: 1]

The emergence of artificial intelligence therefore presents humanity with a paradox.[cite: 1] The more successful humans become at creating systems that imitate aspects of intelligence, the more urgently they must recover the dimensions of humanity that cannot be reduced to intelligence alone.[cite: 1] The future will not be determined merely by whether machines become capable of reasoning.[cite: 1] It will be determined by whether human beings remain capable of responsibility.[cite: 1]

Perhaps the deepest lesson of artificial intelligence is that humanity does not need to prove that it is superior to machines.[cite: 1] It needs to rediscover why human existence matters beyond superiority itself.[cite: 1] The task ahead is not the construction of a world in which technology replaces human limitation.[cite: 1] It is the cultivation of a humanity mature enough to carry the immense responsibility created by its own technological achievements.[cite: 1]

Beyond homo technicus lies not a rejection of technology, but a recovery of the human capacity to respond.[cite: 1] The future of intelligence, artificial or otherwise, ultimately depends on whether humanity can preserve the moral depth required to know what intelligence is for.[cite: 1]
