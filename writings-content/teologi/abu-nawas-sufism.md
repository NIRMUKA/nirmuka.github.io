Abu Nawas, Sufism, and the Existential Presence of God:

An Inquiry into Sin, Repentance, and the Human Search for Divine Meaning

The Paradox of Abu Nawas: The Sinner Who Searches for God

The figure of Abu Nawas presents one of the most intriguing paradoxes in
the history of Islamic intellectual and spiritual imagination. Few
literary figures embody such a profound tension between worldly desire
and spiritual longing, between human weakness and divine aspiration,
between the experience of sin and the hope for mercy. In popular memory,
Abu Nawas is frequently remembered as a witty poet, a figure of humor,
and a symbol of cleverness within the cultural landscape of the Abbasid
period. Yet this familiar image, while historically influential,
conceals a deeper philosophical question. How can a person associated
with pleasure, excess, and human imperfection become remembered through
some of the most moving expressions of repentance and longing for God?
The paradox is not accidental. It reveals something fundamental about
the human condition itself.

The significance of Abu Nawas does not lie merely in the contrast
between a sinful past and a repentant future. Such a reading would
reduce his life to a simple moral narrative in which wrongdoing is
followed by correction. The deeper meaning lies in the fact that Abu
Nawas represents a human being who becomes conscious of the distance
between what he is and what he ought to become. His spiritual
significance emerges precisely because he does not appear as a figure
who has escaped human weakness. He appears as someone who remains within
the struggle of being human. The tension between desire and devotion,
between attachment to the world and awareness of divine reality, becomes
the central drama of his existence.

This dimension places Abu Nawas within a broader existential question
that has occupied both Islamic spirituality and Western philosophy: what
does it mean for a finite and imperfect being to relate to an infinite
and perfect God? Human beings experience themselves as incomplete. They
are aware of their limitations, contradictions, failures, and desires.
Yet this awareness itself creates the possibility of transcendence. The
human being is not merely a creature that fails; the human being is a
creature that recognizes failure and asks whether failure is the final
truth about existence.

In this sense, Abu Nawas becomes philosophically important because he
challenges a simplistic understanding of spirituality. Spiritual life is
often imagined as the achievement of purity, as though the search for
God begins only after human imperfections have been overcome. The figure
of Abu Nawas suggests a different possibility. The search for God may
begin precisely through the recognition of imperfection. The awareness
of distance from God can itself become the beginning of movement toward
God.

The existential dimension of Abu Nawas therefore does not depend on
whether he represents a perfectly consistent spiritual model. Rather,
his importance lies in the honesty of his confrontation with human
weakness. He does not present the human being as a completed moral
achievement. He presents the human being as a journey, a being caught
between what is and what should be, between the limits of earthly
existence and the desire for divine meaning.

This understanding brings Abu Nawas into conversation with the broader
tradition of Sufism. Sufism has often emphasized that the greatest
obstacle between human beings and God is not simply external wrongdoing,
but the illusion of self-sufficiency. The human being becomes
spiritually distant from God when the self becomes convinced that it can
exist independently of the Divine. Repentance, therefore, is not merely
the rejection of certain actions. It is a transformation in the way the
human being understands itself.

The prayer attributed to Abu Nawas, especially the famous expression of
humility before God, reflects this deeper awareness. Its power does not
come from a denial of sin but from the confession of dependence. The
speaker does not approach God by claiming moral superiority. He
approaches through vulnerability. The human being stands before the
Divine not as someone who possesses perfection, but as someone who
recognizes the impossibility of achieving perfection without divine
mercy.

Here lies the philosophical depth of Abu Nawas. He represents not the
opposite of spirituality, but one of its most human forms. The spiritual
journey begins not with the illusion that humans are already close to
God, but with the realization that they are not. The awareness of
separation becomes the condition for seeking reunion.

Beyond the Image of the Court Poet: Abu Nawas as an Existential Figure

To understand Abu Nawas philosophically, it is necessary to move beyond
the simplified image of the court poet who merely represents pleasure
and worldly enjoyment. Such a representation captures only one dimension
of his literary persona. Like many significant historical figures, Abu
Nawas cannot be reduced to a single identity. He existed within a
cultural environment where poetry, politics, religion, pleasure, and
intellectual debate interacted in complex ways. His apparent
contradictions are not merely personal characteristics. They reflect a
deeper condition of human existence.

The Abbasid period in which Abu Nawas lived was characterized by
remarkable intellectual and cultural diversity. It was a time when
scientific inquiry, philosophical translation, literary innovation,
theological debate, and political transformation developed alongside
questions of religious authority and personal morality. Within such an
environment, the human being was increasingly understood as a subject
capable of knowledge, desire, creativity, and moral responsibility. Abu
Nawas's poetry emerged from this world of complexity, where the
boundaries between earthly experience and spiritual aspiration were
constantly negotiated.

The existential importance of Abu Nawas lies in the fact that he does
not fit comfortably into categories. He cannot be understood simply as a
saint, but neither can he be understood merely as a sinner. He
represents the unstable middle condition in which most human beings
actually exist. Human life is rarely characterized by complete virtue or
complete corruption. It is shaped by contradiction, movement, and
struggle.

This observation connects Abu Nawas with a central concern of
existential philosophy: human beings are beings who must constantly
interpret themselves. A person is not identical with past actions alone.
The meaning of a life cannot be reduced to isolated moments because
human existence involves possibility, transformation, and orientation
toward the future. The person who has failed is not merely a failed
person. The person who recognizes failure and seeks transformation
enters a different existential relationship with himself and with God.

Within Islamic spirituality, this idea appears through the concept of
tawbah, or repentance. Repentance is not simply a legal correction of
wrongdoing. It represents a turning of the whole person toward a
different orientation of existence. The Arabic root of the word conveys
the idea of returning. This return is not merely movement from one
behaviour to another. It is a return of the self toward its proper
relation with the Divine.

Abu Nawas becomes significant because his spiritual expression emerges
from precisely this movement. His longing for forgiveness is powerful
because it does not emerge from an abstract moral position. It emerges
from the recognition of human insufficiency. His appeal to God is not
the voice of someone who believes himself worthy of divine acceptance.
It is the voice of someone who understands that human worth cannot
ultimately be grounded in human achievement alone.

This point distinguishes the Sufi understanding of God from a purely
juridical understanding of religion. While moral responsibility remains
essential, Sufi thought often emphasizes the transformative relationship
between divine mercy and human consciousness. The human being does not
approach God only through fear of punishment or expectation of reward.
The deeper movement involves love, recognition, and awareness of
dependence.

The existential significance of Abu Nawas can therefore be understood
through this tension. He is not important because he represents a
perfect human being. He is important because he represents the human
being who becomes aware of the need for perfection beyond himself. His
weakness becomes the place where spiritual awareness emerges.

The philosophical significance of sin within the spiritual tradition
cannot be understood merely as a violation of a moral rule. Such an
interpretation, although containing an important element of truth, does
not fully capture the existential depth of the human encounter with
wrongdoing. Sin is not only an action that breaks a commandment; it is
also an experience through which human beings become aware of their own
limitations, divided desires, and distance from the source of meaning.
The language of sin therefore belongs not only to ethics but also to
anthropology, because it reveals a particular understanding of what kind
of beings humans are. Human beings are creatures capable of recognizing
the good while simultaneously failing to fully embody it. They are
beings who can understand transcendence while remaining attached to
finite desires.

This tension is central to the spiritual meaning of Abu Nawas. His
importance does not arise because he represents an ideal moral
condition. On the contrary, his significance emerges from his awareness
that he does not possess such a condition. The person who has never
experienced moral conflict may possess innocence, but the person who
recognizes his own weakness enters a different kind of spiritual
awareness. The recognition of failure can become the beginning of
transformation because it removes the illusion that the human self is
complete and independent.

This point has parallels with Augustine's understanding of human nature.
For Augustine, the problem of humanity was not simply that individuals
commit particular wrong actions. The deeper problem was that human
desire itself can become disordered when it turns away from its proper
orientation toward God. The human being seeks happiness, but often seeks
it through things that cannot provide lasting fulfillment. The problem
is not that human beings desire, but that they desire in the wrong
direction. The heart becomes restless because it seeks infinite
satisfaction through finite objects.

Although Abu Nawas belongs to a different intellectual and cultural
context, a similar existential structure appears in his spiritual
expression. His recognition of sin is not merely an admission of having
broken rules. It is the recognition that worldly pleasures, however
attractive, cannot ultimately answer the deepest longing of the human
soul. The problem of human existence is not simply that people commit
errors. The deeper problem is that they often attempt to find ultimate
meaning in things that are incapable of carrying such weight.

This perspective is especially important within Sufi thought. Many Sufi
thinkers did not approach human imperfection with simple moral
condemnation. They understood the human being as a creature on a journey
toward God. The distance between humanity and the Divine was not merely
an obstacle but also the condition that made spiritual movement
possible. Without awareness of distance, there would be no longing.
Without longing, there would be no journey.

The concept of tawbah illustrates this dynamic. Repentance is often
misunderstood as a return to moral correctness after an error. Yet in
its deeper spiritual sense, tawbah represents a transformation of
orientation. The individual does not merely stop performing certain
actions. The individual turns the entire direction of existence toward
God. Repentance is therefore not only about what the person has done; it
is about what kind of person the individual is becoming.

This existential dimension explains why the figure of Abu Nawas remains
compelling. His spiritual voice is powerful because it emerges from
vulnerability. He does not approach God as someone who can present a
record of moral achievements. He approaches as someone who understands
his dependence upon divine mercy. The relationship between human beings
and God is not established through human perfection, because perfection
belongs only to God. The relationship begins through recognition of
dependence.

Here a significant philosophical question appears: does awareness of
imperfection separate humans from God, or can it become the very
condition through which humans encounter God? The Sufi answer often
moves toward the second possibility. The recognition of human limitation
can destroy the illusion of self-sufficiency, opening the possibility of
a deeper relationship with the Divine. The awareness of weakness becomes
spiritually meaningful because it directs the individual beyond the
self.

This idea also resonates with Søren Kierkegaard's analysis of despair.
For Kierkegaard, despair is not merely sadness or psychological
suffering. It is a condition in which the self fails to become itself in
relation to the source that grounds it. The human being can possess
external success, knowledge, and worldly achievement while remaining
internally disconnected from the foundation of existence. True selfhood
emerges when the individual recognizes the relationship between the
finite self and the infinite.

Although Kierkegaard developed his philosophy within a Christian
framework, his existential concern shares an important affinity with
Islamic mystical thought. Both traditions recognize that the human self
cannot fully understand itself without reference to transcendence. The
self becomes clearer not by turning exclusively inward, but by
discovering its dependence upon something beyond itself.

Abu Nawas's spiritual significance can therefore be understood through
this existential structure. His confession before God is not simply the
confession of guilt. It is the revelation of a deeper human condition.
The human being is a creature who desires infinity while existing within
limitation. Humans seek permanence while experiencing change, seek
perfection while carrying weakness, and seek meaning while confronting
uncertainty.

The existence of God, from this perspective, is not merely a theological
proposition to be accepted intellectually. It becomes an existential
reality encountered through the human experience of dependence, longing,
and transformation. God is not approached only as an explanation of the
universe but as the answer to the deepest question of human existence:
why does the finite human being continue to seek what exceeds the
finite?

The Sufi Understanding of God: From Proof to Encounter

The question of God's existence has occupied philosophy for centuries.
Classical philosophical traditions often approached the problem through
rational demonstration. Thinkers developed arguments concerning
causality, necessity, order, and the nature of existence itself. These
approaches remain important because they attempt to examine whether
belief in God can be intellectually justified. Yet Sufi thought
introduces another dimension into the discussion. The question is not
only whether God exists as a metaphysical reality. The deeper question
is how the human being encounters that reality.

This distinction is essential for understanding the spiritual meaning of
Abu Nawas. His relationship with God is not expressed primarily through
abstract philosophical argument. His poetry does not attempt to
construct a proof of divine existence. Instead, it expresses the
existential experience of a human being who recognizes that his own
existence points beyond itself. The presence of God becomes meaningful
not only as an object of belief but as a reality that transforms the
person who encounters it.

This does not mean that Sufism rejects rational reflection. Many Sufi
thinkers were deeply engaged with philosophy, theology, and intellectual
inquiry. Figures such as Al-Ghazali demonstrated that spiritual
experience and philosophical reasoning need not be enemies. Yet Sufi
thought often argues that knowledge of God cannot be reduced entirely to
conceptual understanding. Knowing about God and knowing God are not
identical.

The distinction resembles the difference between knowing a person
through information and knowing a person through relationship. One may
possess numerous facts about someone without truly encountering that
person. Similarly, one may possess theological concepts about God
without experiencing the transformative awareness of divine presence.
The Sufi tradition emphasizes that the deepest knowledge of God involves
a transformation of the knower.

This perspective changes the meaning of divine existence. God is not
merely the highest object within a hierarchy of beings. God is
understood as the ultimate source upon which all beings depend. The
human encounter with God therefore involves a transformation in
self-understanding. The individual discovers that existence is not
self-grounded. The self is received before it is created by its own
choices.

In this sense, Abu Nawas's repentance represents more than a request for
forgiveness. It represents an encounter between human finitude and
divine infinitude. The human being recognizes that worldly identity,
reputation, pleasure, and achievement cannot provide the final
foundation of existence. What remains is the relationship with the
Divine.

The philosophical depth of this relationship lies in its paradox. The
human being becomes most aware of himself when he recognizes that he is
not sufficient by himself. Dependence does not eliminate human dignity;
it reveals the condition through which dignity becomes possible. The
finite becomes meaningful precisely because it participates in a reality
greater than itself.

Abu Nawas therefore represents a form of existential theology in which
God is not merely an answer to intellectual uncertainty but the horizon
within which human existence becomes intelligible. His poetry reveals a
fundamental insight: the search for God is inseparable from the search
for the true meaning of being human.

Abu Nawas and Divine Mercy: Why Does God Forgive the Imperfect Human
Being?

The question of divine forgiveness contains one of the deepest
philosophical problems within religious thought: why would an absolutely
perfect and infinite God establish a relationship with imperfect and
finite human beings? At first glance, the relationship between human
sinfulness and divine perfection appears to contain a tension. If God
represents absolute goodness, justice, and perfection, then human
failure seems to create an almost unbridgeable distance between the
Creator and the creature. The human being violates moral expectations,
repeatedly fails to achieve spiritual ideals, and remains vulnerable to
desires that contradict higher aspirations. Yet religious traditions,
particularly mystical traditions, insist that this distance does not
represent the end of the relationship between humanity and God. Instead,
it becomes the very space in which divine mercy becomes visible.

The spiritual significance of Abu Nawas emerges precisely within this
tension. His famous expressions of repentance are powerful because they
do not attempt to deny human imperfection. He does not approach God by
presenting himself as someone who has achieved moral purity. His appeal
is based on the opposite recognition: that the human being remains
incomplete and dependent upon divine compassion. The philosophical depth
of this attitude lies in the fact that it rejects a transactional
understanding of the relationship between God and humanity. God's
forgiveness is not presented as a reward that humans obtain after
achieving sufficient moral success. It is understood as an expression of
divine generosity that makes transformation possible in the first place.

This point is central to the Sufi understanding of divine mercy. In many
mystical traditions, the relationship between God and human beings is
not primarily described through legal categories alone, but through the
language of love, proximity, and transformation. The human being does
not approach God because the human being has become worthy in an
absolute sense. Rather, the human being approaches God because God is
the source of mercy that allows the imperfect being to return. The
direction of movement is therefore not simply from human effort toward
divine acceptance. It is also from divine mercy toward human
transformation.

This understanding challenges a common assumption about spiritual
development. Many people imagine that the search for God requires first
becoming morally complete, as if perfection is the condition for
approaching the Divine. Sufi thought often reverses this logic. The
recognition of imperfection is not necessarily an obstacle to spiritual
life. It can become its beginning. The person who recognizes his own
limitations has already moved beyond the illusion of self-sufficiency.
He has discovered a truth about existence: that the human being cannot
become complete through itself alone.

In this sense, Abu Nawas represents a profound theological anthropology.
He reveals a vision of humanity in which weakness is not the final
definition of the person. Human beings are not identical with their
failures because existence itself remains open to transformation. A
person who has sinned can still become a person oriented toward God. The
past does not disappear, but it can be reinterpreted through repentance.
The meaning of human existence is not determined only by what has
occurred, but also by the direction toward which the person turns.

This idea has a deep connection with Al-Ghazali's understanding of
spiritual transformation. For Al-Ghazali, the human problem is not
merely ignorance of religious rules. The deeper problem is the condition
of the heart. The human being can possess knowledge while remaining
distant from truth because knowledge alone does not transform the
orientation of the self. Spiritual renewal requires a reordering of
desire, a purification of intention, and a movement from attachment to
the created world toward awareness of the Creator.

Abu Nawas's existential significance can be understood within this
framework. His repentance is not merely a confession that certain
actions were wrong. It represents the awakening of a person who realizes
that worldly pleasures cannot carry the weight of ultimate meaning. The
problem with excessive attachment to the world is not that the world
itself lacks value. Rather, the problem occurs when finite things are
expected to provide what only the infinite can provide.

Here the question of divine mercy becomes inseparable from the question
of human desire. Human beings naturally seek happiness, fulfillment, and
permanence. Yet they often search for these realities in temporary
objects and experiences. The Sufi tradition interprets this condition
not simply as moral failure but as a misunderstanding of the structure
of existence. The human heart desires the infinite because it was not
created to be satisfied entirely by finite things.

The importance of divine mercy is therefore not merely that it removes
punishment. Mercy restores the proper relationship between the human
being and reality itself. It returns the person from illusion to
awareness, from self-centeredness to dependence, from separation to
connection. Forgiveness becomes an ontological transformation because it
changes the way the person understands his own existence.

The concept of divine forgiveness also introduces a classical
philosophical tension between justice and mercy. If God is perfectly
just, how can forgiveness be granted to those who have failed morally?
If wrongdoing has consequences, does forgiveness compromise justice?
Conversely, if God forgives without limitation, does morality lose its
seriousness?

These questions have occupied theologians and philosophers across
different traditions. The difficulty arises because human concepts of
justice often operate within systems of equivalence. A wrong action
demands a corresponding consequence. A debt requires repayment. A
violation requires correction. These principles are essential for social
order. Yet divine mercy introduces a different dimension, one that
cannot be fully understood through human legal categories.

The Sufi perspective often approaches this tension by distinguishing
between human justice and divine reality. Human justice is necessary
because human beings exist within limited social conditions. Societies
require accountability because human relationships depend upon trust and
responsibility. Yet divine mercy reveals that existence itself cannot be
reduced to a system of exchange. The relationship between God and
humanity exceeds calculation.

Abu Nawas's spiritual expression reflects this understanding. He does
not argue that his failures should be ignored. Instead, he acknowledges
them while appealing to a reality greater than his own shortcomings. His
hope is not based on denying justice but on recognizing that divine
mercy is not limited by human categories of merit.

This distinction is philosophically significant because it changes the
meaning of repentance. If forgiveness were merely the cancellation of
punishment, repentance would remain primarily a legal event. But if
forgiveness represents transformation, then repentance concerns the
reconstruction of the self. The person who receives mercy is not simply
returned to a previous condition. The experience of mercy changes how
the person understands himself.

This idea appears throughout mystical thought. Ibn Arabi, for example,
emphasized the depth of the relationship between divine reality and
creation. The human being is not an isolated entity existing
independently from God. Existence itself is continuously dependent upon
the Divine. From this perspective, returning to God is not simply moving
toward an external authority. It is rediscovering the deepest truth
about one's own existence.

Abu Nawas becomes a powerful symbol of this movement because his
spirituality begins from honesty. He does not construct an idealized
image of himself before God. He does not claim innocence where there is
weakness. Instead, he presents the human condition as it is: conflicted,
vulnerable, and dependent. The paradox is that this very honesty becomes
a form of spiritual knowledge.

The person who believes himself perfect has little reason to seek
transformation. The person who recognizes his incompleteness becomes
open to change. Therefore, awareness of sin can paradoxically become
closer to God than the illusion of moral superiority. The awareness of
distance creates the desire for proximity.

This is one of the deepest insights contained within Abu Nawas's
spiritual legacy. The human being does not encounter God after escaping
humanity. The encounter occurs within humanity itself, with all its
contradictions and limitations. The Divine is not found only by those
who have eliminated weakness, but also by those who recognize their
weakness and turn beyond themselves.

From an existential perspective, repentance represents one of the most
profound forms of human transformation. It is not simply a change of
behaviour. It is a change in the relationship between the self, the
world, and the ultimate source of meaning. A person who repents does not
merely regret a past action. The person reinterprets the meaning of his
own life.

This understanding places repentance close to the existential concept of
conversion. In existential philosophy, transformation occurs when
individuals experience a fundamental shift in how they understand
themselves. The world may remain the same externally, but the person's
relationship with that world changes. What was previously considered
valuable may lose its importance, while what was previously ignored may
become central.

Abu Nawas represents this transformation because his movement toward God
is not presented as the achievement of an abstract ideal. It is the
movement of a concrete human being who has experienced desire, pleasure,
failure, and limitation. His spiritual journey demonstrates that human
beings do not become meaningful by becoming less human. They become
meaningful by understanding human existence more deeply.

The figure of Abu Nawas allows us to reconsider one of the oldest
assumptions within religious thought: that the human search for God
begins with the achievement of moral perfection. Many religious and
philosophical traditions have emphasized discipline, purification, and
transformation as essential movements toward the Divine. Yet the
spiritual significance of Abu Nawas emerges from a different starting
point. His journey does not begin with perfection but with recognition
of imperfection. He does not represent the human being who has already
overcome weakness and therefore becomes worthy of divine attention. He
represents the human being who discovers that weakness itself reveals a
deeper truth about existence: that the human being is not
self-sufficient.

This insight may be described as an existential theology of
imperfection. Such a perspective does not glorify sin, nor does it
minimize moral responsibility. It does not claim that failure is
spiritually desirable. Rather, it argues that the awareness of failure
can become the condition through which human beings encounter their
deepest dependence upon God. Imperfection becomes meaningful not because
imperfection itself is valuable, but because the recognition of
imperfection removes the illusion that human beings can become complete
through themselves.

The philosophical importance of Abu Nawas lies precisely in this
paradox. He is remembered not because he represents an uncomplicated
model of holiness, but because he reveals the complexity of the human
relationship with the Divine. The human being is neither pure nor
entirely corrupt. Human existence unfolds between aspiration and
limitation, between the desire for transcendence and the reality of
finitude. This intermediate condition is not an accidental feature of
human life. It is the very structure through which the question of God
becomes meaningful.

A being that already possesses complete self-sufficiency would have no
existential need for transcendence. The search for God emerges because
human beings experience a distance within themselves. They recognize
possibilities that they cannot fully realize, ideals that they cannot
completely embody, and meanings that they cannot entirely create. The
human being is therefore characterized by a tension between what is
given and what is desired, between present reality and ultimate
possibility.

In this sense, Abu Nawas represents a distinctly human form of
spirituality. His movement toward God does not arise from confidence in
his own achievement. It arises from the awareness that achievement alone
cannot resolve the deepest human question. Wealth, pleasure, reputation,
and intellectual ability may enrich human existence, but they cannot
provide the ultimate foundation upon which existence rests. The human
heart continues to search because finite realities cannot fully answer
an infinite longing.

This perspective has important implications for understanding the nature
of religious experience. If spirituality were only the achievement of
moral perfection, then religion would become primarily a project of
self-improvement. The individual would approach God as someone
attempting to become worthy through personal effort. Yet many mystical
traditions reverse this relationship. The individual approaches God
precisely because personal effort is insufficient. Human transformation
begins not from the confidence of self-mastery, but from humility before
a reality greater than the self.

The concept of humility in Sufi thought is therefore not simply a moral
virtue. It represents a philosophical recognition about the nature of
existence. The human being is dependent, contingent, and limited. To
acknowledge this condition is not to diminish humanity. It is to
understand humanity more accurately. The problem is not that humans are
imperfect. The problem begins when humans deny their imperfection and
attempt to occupy a position of absolute independence.

One of the deepest contributions of existential thought is the rejection
of the idea that human beings can be understood as fixed entities with
completed identities. Human existence is characterized by movement. A
person is not merely what has already happened but also what remains
possible. The individual exists through choices, interpretations, and
orientations toward the future.

This understanding creates an important connection between existential
philosophy and Sufi anthropology. Both perspectives understand the human
being as a journey rather than a finished product. The self is not
something simply discovered as an already completed reality. It is
something gradually formed through the relationship between the
individual, the world, and the transcendent source of meaning.

Abu Nawas embodies this dynamic character of human existence. His
significance does not depend upon whether every aspect of his life can
be reconciled into a perfectly consistent moral narrative. The deeper
point is that human beings themselves are rarely consistent in this way.
Human existence contains contradiction because human beings possess the
capacity to recognize higher possibilities while remaining vulnerable to
lower desires.

This contradiction is often treated as a weakness of human nature. Yet
existential theology interprets it differently. The contradiction
reveals the openness of human existence. A being that is completely
determined by instinct could not experience moral struggle. A being that
has no awareness of higher possibilities could not experience regret or
repentance. The very possibility of transformation depends upon the fact
that humans are not identical with their immediate desires.

Repentance therefore becomes a confirmation of human freedom. The person
who repents demonstrates that the past does not completely determine the
future. Human beings are capable of turning, changing direction, and
reinterpreting their own existence. The possibility of return exists
because human identity remains open.

This understanding also changes the meaning of divine forgiveness.
Forgiveness is not merely the removal of a negative record. It is the
recognition that the human being cannot be reduced to the worst moment
of his existence. Divine mercy opens the possibility that a person's
future may exceed his past.

In this sense, forgiveness reveals a profound truth about human
identity. If human beings were defined entirely by their failures,
transformation would be impossible. Yet spiritual traditions affirm that
the person who falls can rise, the person who loses direction can
return, and the person who experiences distance can seek proximity
again.

Abu Nawas becomes a symbol of this possibility. He represents the human
being who discovers that the path toward God does not require pretending
to have never been lost. The path begins when the individual recognizes
being lost and seeks direction.

The existential reading of Abu Nawas also challenges the tendency to
understand religion primarily through moral achievement. Moral
transformation remains essential, but the deepest religious relationship
cannot be reduced to a system of achievement and reward. The
relationship between God and humanity is not comparable to a contract
between equal parties. The infinite and the finite do not meet on
symmetrical terms.

This asymmetry is the foundation of divine mercy. God does not forgive
because humans have become equal to divine perfection. God forgives
because divine reality exceeds human limitation. The possibility of
forgiveness exists because the relationship originates not only from
human movement toward God but from divine generosity toward humanity.

Such an understanding does not weaken moral responsibility. On the
contrary, it strengthens it. A person who recognizes divine mercy does
not become indifferent toward wrongdoing. The awareness of mercy creates
gratitude, and gratitude transforms the way a person relates to actions,
desires, and others. The individual does not pursue goodness merely to
avoid punishment but because goodness becomes a response to divine
generosity.

Here the spiritual meaning of Abu Nawas becomes clearer. His confession
before God is not an expression of hopelessness. It is an expression of
hope grounded in humility. He does not deny his limitations, but neither
does he allow those limitations to become the final definition of his
existence.

This creates a profound existential lesson. Human beings often attempt
to escape their imperfection by creating images of themselves as
complete, successful, and independent. Yet such attempts frequently
produce anxiety because they are built upon an illusion. The human being
cannot remove finitude from existence. Mortality, uncertainty, and
limitation remain permanent dimensions of human life.

The alternative offered by spiritual traditions is not the elimination
of finitude but the transformation of the relationship toward finitude.
The person who accepts dependence can experience peace where the person
seeking absolute independence experiences anxiety. The person who
acknowledges limitation can discover a deeper form of freedom because he
no longer needs to maintain the illusion of perfection.

Abu Nawas as Homo Spiritualis: The Human Being Who Returns

The lasting significance of Abu Nawas can therefore be understood
through the concept of homo spiritualis, the spiritual human being. This
does not refer to a person who has escaped ordinary human existence. It
refers to a person who discovers a deeper dimension within ordinary
existence. The spiritual human being is not someone who no longer
struggles. It is someone who understands the meaning of the struggle.

Abu Nawas represents a humanity that remains unfinished. He reminds us
that spiritual life is not the story of perfect beings descending into
the world. It is the story of imperfect beings searching for the source
of perfection. The movement toward God is meaningful because the human
being experiences distance.

This perspective also provides a different answer to the philosophical
question of why God matters. The existence of God is not meaningful
merely because God provides an explanation for the origin of the
universe. For existential theology, God matters because the human being
discovers that existence itself points beyond self-contained
explanations. Human beings experience a longing for meaning that cannot
be completely satisfied by finite achievements.

The question of God emerges from within human existence itself. It
arises from the awareness that humans are more than what they
immediately possess, more than what they accomplish, and more than what
they can measure. The longing for God is therefore not an escape from
humanity. It is an expression of humanity's deepest condition.

Abu Nawas reveals this truth through the paradox of his life and poetry.
The person who appears distant from God becomes the person who most
clearly expresses the longing for divine closeness. The person who
recognizes his weakness becomes the person who understands the need for
mercy. The person who acknowledges his incompleteness becomes the person
open to transformation.

The existential theology of imperfection therefore offers a final
insight: human beings do not encounter God after they become perfect.
They encounter God when they understand that perfection cannot be
achieved without God.

The deepest spiritual movement is not from human perfection toward
divine acceptance. It is from human awareness of limitation toward
divine relationship.

Abu Nawas stands as a reminder that the search for God begins not with
the denial of human weakness, but with the courage to face it. In that
confrontation, the human being discovers not the end of dignity, but its
foundation. The imperfect human being becomes capable of approaching the
perfect Divine precisely because he recognizes that he is not the source
of his own completeness.

And perhaps this is the enduring philosophical meaning of Abu Nawas:
that the distance between humanity and God is not merely an obstacle to
overcome. It is the space in which longing, repentance, and spiritual
transformation become possible.
